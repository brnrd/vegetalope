Does being a Head of Engineering mean stepping away from the code and the teams’ technical work?

My responsibilities have changed, and there’s less time to pick up tickets. But I still want some recent, first-hand experience of what it takes to get a change into production.

Toyota has a practice called genchi genbutsu, meaning “go and see for yourself”. It’s part of the thinking behind the Toyota Production System, which Taiichi Ohno helped develop. At Toyota, managers are expected to spend time on the factory floor, observing the process and talking to the people doing the work before deciding what to improve.

I have a soft spot for this idea, alongside kanban.

In practice, that can mean investigating a bug or occasionally taking a small change through the whole process: understanding the ticket, setting up locally, running tests, getting a review, deploying and checking the result in production.

The code is only part of what I want to understand. Which permissions are missing? How long do tests take? Who can approve a release?

I want to follow the same process as the engineers I work with, from onboarding and access requests to tooling and release. Following the README in an unfamiliar codebase can reveal missing prerequisites, outdated steps or knowledge nobody has written down.

That gives me useful context for conversations with EMs about delivery. “The team is moving slowly” becomes a discussion about where time is going, what keeps interrupting people and which constraints we can change.

Some problems need support across the organisation. Sometimes it helps simply to share what another team has put in place to solve a similar problem.

At my current company, EMs are expected to stay hands-on. Conversations with EMs at other companies have shown me how much the role can vary: some roles combine EM and Tech Lead responsibilities, others leave less room for coding. Pairing or following a change through the workflow can provide useful exposure while the engineer keeps ownership.

My experience would still be one sample. I would compare it with the team’s feedback and delivery data to understand whether others are running into the same difficulties.

That’s what I value in genchi genbutsu: a clearer understanding of the work as it happens today. It gives us a better starting point for discussing what would help the team.
