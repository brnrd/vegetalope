When a developer tells me they’d like to become an engineering manager one day, I want to help them understand what the job involves. I encourage them to read about it, talk to existing engineering managers and take on more responsibility over time. Leading a piece of work or helping colleagues develop gives them useful experience, as well as a better idea of whether they enjoy that kind of work.

Some parts are harder to practise before you have the job. Leading a project gives you some exposure, but managing someone’s performance and handling difficult conversations call for different skills. Imagining themselves in those situations helps them recognise what they already bring and what they need to work on next.

I’ve put together a neutral version of the written exercise I use in our internal promotion process for developers applying for an engineering management position. It gives someone a way to explore the role at their own pace, without applying for a position. The questions cover different parts of management, including things they may not have considered when they first expressed an interest.

I’m happy for them to use AI to explore the questions and prepare their answers. Reading a book, talking to an engineering manager or working through a situation with AI are all useful ways to understand the role. What matters is whether they understand their answers, can explain their reasoning and can rethink their approach when the context changes.

I want them to come away with a clearer idea of what they’re getting into. They might feel more interested in management, or they might have doubts they didn’t have before. Either helps us work out where they want to go and how I can support them.

In the internal promotion process itself, the written exercise is followed by an interview. We discuss the candidate’s answers and work through two live role plays focused on people management. I don’t expect them to behave like an experienced manager. They haven’t done the job yet.

The role plays help make this concrete. Responding to another person, even in a simulation, tells us more than a written answer alone. It’s still only a glimpse of how someone might handle the job, but it helps us understand their starting point and where they would need support.

Beyond their answers, I want to understand what draws them to management and the values that would guide them. I’m interested in how they connect with people, listen and bring others with them. Can they acknowledge what they don’t know, consider another point of view and ask for help? What gives me confidence is someone who wants to help people do well, understands the responsibility that comes with the role and is ready to keep learning. We can start building on those qualities long before there’s a management role to fill.
