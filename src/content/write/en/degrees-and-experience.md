Does a degree matter?

It's an old debate in software engineering. How much can formal education prepare us for the realities of the job, and how much do we learn through experience?

I recently watched Teacher's Pet, a 1958 film starring Clark Gable and Doris Day, and was surprised by how familiar its central argument felt. Gable plays a city editor of a large metropolitan newspaper who worked his way up without formal education and has little patience with people who believe journalism can be taught in a classroom. Day plays a university professor of journalism who disagrees with him.

But the film gets more interesting when it stops being about education. Gable's character isn't just defending experience. He's defending the story he tells himself about his success. He made it without university, so acknowledging its value would mean reconsidering something he's spent years dismissing. The professor has her own blind spots: her idealised view of journalism overlooks some of the commercial realities of running a newspaper. Both have something to learn.

I've thought about this a lot when hiring engineers. I rarely pay attention to where someone studied, or whether they have a degree. I care much more about what they can do, their attitude, curiosity and ambition. One of the most revealing moments in an interview is when I ask something they don't know. Do they bluff, shut down, or admit they don't know and try to reason through it?

I've seen engineers who entered the profession after a short training course become every bit as capable as colleagues with a Master's degree. That doesn't make academic education worthless. Qualifications and capability aren't the same thing, and the route someone took shouldn't determine how we judge their potential.

The same applies as we gain experience. Years of decisions, incidents and successes help us recognise patterns and avoid mistakes. But they can also reinforce our assumptions. A colleague proposing a different approach can feel like a criticism of how we've worked for years. I've seen that tension between engineers who understand why a legacy system works the way it does and those who look at it with fresh eyes. Often, both see something the other misses.

For managers, understanding why someone becomes defensive or dismissive doesn't mean excusing it. It helps us approach the conversation differently, especially when what they're defending is tied to their sense of competence.

What I liked about the end of the film is that both characters act on what they've learned. The editor encourages a younger colleague to continue his education, while the professor invites the editor himself to share his practical experience with her students. Neither abandons their beliefs. They make room for what the other knows.

Perhaps that's the real test of experience: not how firmly we defend what we've learned, but whether we're still willing to change our minds when someone shows us something we've missed.
