How do we decide whether someone is doing their job well or going beyond what’s expected? After mid-year or annual reviews, managers meet for calibration to compare their assessments. We need to explain what someone did, why it mattered and why it deserves a particular rating.

I’ve written before about encouraging the people I manage to keep their own record of contributions. A “brag document” is simply a running list of what you’ve done and the difference it made, with dates and evidence. It gives you and your manager a clearer picture to discuss when the details of six months’ work start to fade.

My manager recently introduced a practice we’re trialling: a 30-minute meeting every fortnight to discuss outstanding contributions. Each example comes with what happened, evidence of the impact, and a question: what would we normally expect from someone at that level?

After a couple of sessions, I’ve noticed how our experience, our understanding of the role and our technical background, whether web or mobile, shape what we see as routine or extraordinary. We can also be tempted to put someone forward as exceeding expectations because “everyone knows they’re good”. That may be true, but sometimes all we have is a shared impression. We still need examples of what they did and how it went beyond what’s expected in their role.

We can also see something as extraordinary when it’s actually part of the job. Helping another team resolve a difficult technical decision might be a real stretch for one engineer and an expected part of a staff engineer’s role.

That’s why we need clear job descriptions and career paths, with a shared understanding of what’s expected at each level. Concrete examples help us discuss what goes beyond those expectations. Keeping them over time also helps us recognise when someone does that consistently, even after we’ve become used to it.

The trial has already prompted interesting discussions about where our judgements differ and what counts as extraordinary. Having those conversations throughout the year should make reviews and calibration much easier, with fewer gaps to fill from memory.

The point is to help people get the recognition their work deserves. When we discuss ratings, pay or promotion, we should be able to explain what someone contributed and make a clear case for rewarding it.
